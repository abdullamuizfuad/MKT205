Question 2 of 7

Explain and elaborate the correlation coefficient.

Show tips
  • Define it: one number that sums up the relationship between two variables.
  • Give its range, −1.00 to +1.00. What does the sign tell you, and what does the size tell you?
  • What does r = 0 mean? (Careful: it means no linear relationship.)
  • Say that r must be statistically significant before you interpret it.
  • Explain why correlation does not prove causation, with an example.

Example: iced drink sales and sunburn cases both rise in hot weather, but one does not cause the other.

Watch out: r = −0.80 is stronger than r = +0.30. The sign only shows direction.

Question 3 of 7

Explain and elaborate covariation.

Show tips
  • Define it: how much change in one variable goes together, consistently, with change in another.
  • Describe the four patterns in the plots: positive, negative, curvilinear, and no relationship.
  • Link it to the other questions: covariation is shown with a scatter diagram (Q4) and measured with a correlation coefficient (Q2).
  • Give a marketing example for each pattern.

Example: as price goes up, units sold go down. That is negative covariation.

Watch out: covariation is about variables moving together, not about one causing the other.

Question 4 of 7

What is a scatter diagram?

Show tips
  • Define it: a graph that plots pairs of values for two variables.
  • What does each dot stand for? Which variable goes on which axis?
  • What can you read from it? Direction, strength (how tight the dots are), shape, and outliers.
  • Draw a small labelled sketch in your answer. It shows you understand.

Example: each dot is one day at a Malé café, with temperature on X and iced coffee sales on Y.

Question 5 of 7

Explain and elaborate the Pearson product moment correlation coefficient.

Show tips
  • When is it used? When both variables are metric (interval or ratio scale).
  • What does it measure? The strength and direction of a linear relationship.
  • Write the formula and explain its parts: covariance divided by the product of the two standard deviations.
  • List its assumptions and limits: linear, sensitive to outliers, can miss curved patterns.
  • Say how it is tested: the null hypothesis is that the population correlation is zero.
r = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) ÷ [ (n − 1) · sₓ · sᵧ ]

Watch out: Pearson's r is not for nominal data like gender or region.

Question 6 of 7

Set out the rules of thumb for the strength of a correlation coefficient.

Show tips
  • Strength depends on the size of r, not its sign.
  • The common marketing research textbook rule uses five bands, each about 0.20 wide.
  • Set them out as a table: range of r → strength label.
  • Add the condition: interpret strength only if r is statistically significant.
  • Other authors use different cut-offs, such as Cohen (1988): .10 small, .30 medium, .50 large. Cite whichever source you use.

Watch out: ±0.85 and −0.85 sit in the same band.

Question 7 of 7

Explain bivariate linear regression.

Show tips
  • "Bivariate" means two variables: one independent (x) is used to predict one dependent (y).
  • Write the equation y = a + bx and explain what a (intercept) and b (slope) mean.
  • How is the line fitted? The least squares method, which minimises the squared residuals.
  • How good is the line? Explain R², and why b must be significant.
  • How is regression different from correlation? Prediction vs association.

Example: use the equation on the right to predict bookings for a given ad budget.

Watch out: don't predict far outside the range of your data.
